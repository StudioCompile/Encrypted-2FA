How Vault protects your data

Vault assumes someone else might get onto your computer. So nothing stored on it can open your data, and nothing is remembered between visits. Here is exactly how that works, and where the limits are.

In plain words

  • Your codes, notes and files are scrambled (encrypted) on your device before they are saved anywhere.
  • Only your passcode or passkey can unscramble them. Vault doesn’t keep either one.
  • Google only confirms who you are. It can’t see your data and can’t unlock it.
  • Vault locks when you leave, so someone else on your computer sees nothing.

The sections below are the technical details, for anyone who wants them.

You unlock it every time

When you close, reload or lock Vault, your keys are erased from memory. To open it again you enter your passcode or use your passkey. There is no “remember me” and no saved password. The passcode field is drawn so browsers don’t offer to save it.

Encryption

Everything you store is encrypted with a random 256-bit data key using AES-256-GCM. Each item has its own random IV, and the item’s id is bound into the encryption so items can’t be swapped or altered unnoticed.

The data key is never stored in the clear. It is stored only wrapped, in up to three ways, and any one of them opens it:

UnlockerHow the wrapping key is made
PasscodePBKDF2-SHA256 with 600,000 iterations and a random salt, then HKDF. Needs at least 10 characters.
Passkey (optional)The WebAuthn PRF extension: your authenticator derives a secret from a salt, only after you verify with biometrics or a PIN. The secret never leaves the authenticator and is never stored, so copying the browser’s files gets an attacker nothing.
Recovery key (Google accounts)A random 160-bit key shown once at setup. It is useless without also being signed in to your Google account.

Changing your passcode re-wraps one key. It doesn’t touch your items.

Google sign-in

Google only proves which account you are, so Vault knows whose encrypted data to fetch. The server checks Google’s signed token (signature, audience, expiry, and a one-time nonce). Google never sees your passcode, keys or data, and signing in with Google does not unlock anything by itself. You still enter your passcode or use your passkey.

With a Google account, the server only releases your wrapped key after a correct passcode proof, and it locks unlocking for 15 minutes after 8 wrong tries. That limits guessing even if someone has your browser session.

No account

The same encryption, but the encrypted data lives only in your browser’s IndexedDB. Nothing is sent anywhere. Because there is no server to rate-limit guesses, a short passcode could be guessed by someone who copies your files, so use a long passcode and add a passkey. Clearing your browser data deletes the vault, so export a backup from Settings.

Other protections

  • Auto-lock after 1 to 30 minutes of inactivity, or as soon as you leave the tab.
  • Session cookies are HttpOnly and SameSite=Strict. Writes from other sites are refused.
  • A strict Content Security Policy. The app page allows only Google’s sign-in script, and nothing else loads from other sites.
  • Backups can be protected with their own passphrase.

Limits to know about

  • While Vault is unlocked, malware or someone controlling your computer remotely can see what you can see. Locking is what protects you. Use short auto-lock times.
  • Not every device supports passkey unlock. It needs the PRF extension, which most current phones, Macs, and security keys have, while support in Windows and some browsers varies. Vault tells you when it’s unavailable, and your passcode always works.
  • Passkeys are tied to the site address you set up on, and a passkey stored only on one device is lost with it. Keep your passcode and recovery key.
  • As with any web app, you trust the site to serve you the right code. If you self-host Vault, that’s you.
  • Keep your 2FA services’ own backup codes. Vault is another copy, not a replacement.
